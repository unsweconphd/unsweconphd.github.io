Most economics theses are three papers, and most of those papers are coauthored and circulating as working papers long before a journal takes them. UNSW has rules about putting that work in your thesis. They turn on **how you use a paper**, not on whether it has been accepted anywhere, and the two that catch people out — your share of the work, and written permission from your coauthors — are far easier to settle when you start a project than in your last month.

Raise this with your supervisor when you start a coauthored paper, and again when you plan your chapters. Your supervisor and the PGC have to approve any paper you include in place of a chapter, so bring uncertain cases to the PGC before you fix your chapter structure.

## What counts as a publication

More than you would expect. UNSW counts work that has been published, submitted for publication, or is in a form suitable for publication. For the Business School that includes:

- a paper published or in press in a journal, or a chapter in a book;
- a paper submitted to a journal and under review;
- a working paper in a series such as SSRN, NBER or CEPR;
- a conference paper in the proceedings, or available as a full paper from the conference programme.

So your job market paper on SSRN counts, even though no journal has taken it. If only the abstract is posted, acknowledge in the chapter that an abstract of the paper is available on SSRN.

A paper you have never circulated is not a publication. You still acknowledge your coauthors — see [acknowledging coauthored work](#acknowledging-coauthored-work).

## The three categories

When you submit, GRIS asks you to declare one category for the whole thesis.

| Category | When it applies |
|---|---|
| 1 | The thesis contains no publications, published or submitted. |
| 2 | Chapters draw on papers, but you have written them up as thesis chapters — rewritten, expanded, or using only part of a paper. You acknowledge the source and your coauthors in the chapter. |
| 3 | At least one chapter **is** a paper, reproduced word for word in place of a chapter. |

One Category 3 chapter makes the whole thesis Category 3, even if every other chapter is an ordinary chapter.

Neither category is the better one. The only question is whether a chapter is the paper itself or your own write-up of it. Rewriting a paper for the thesis — restoring proofs you cut for length, updating the literature, expanding the discussion — is a perfectly good thing to do, and it makes that chapter Category 2.

## Using a paper in place of a chapter

You may do this only if all of the following are true of that paper.

- The research was done during **this** PhD. Work from an earlier degree does not count; if you started the paper during your MPDBS year, check with the PGC before you rely on it.
- You contributed **more than 50%** of the content and are the primary author. Exactly half is not enough.
- You were responsible for planning, executing and preparing the work.
- Nothing stops you including it — no contract, publisher agreement or data agreement.
- You have **written permission from every coauthor**, including any coauthor who is your supervisor.
- Your supervisor and the PGC approve it.
- The paper appears **word for word**, in the author-accepted version — the final draft as accepted, before the publisher's copyediting and typesetting.

Word for word means word for word. If you edit, expand or renumber it to fit the thesis, the chapter is Category 2 instead. You may match the thesis's font and line spacing, and move tables and figures to where they read naturally.

### Alphabetical authorship is not primary authorship

Economics lists authors alphabetically, so being named first says nothing about your contribution, and neither does being named last. What UNSW asks is whether you contributed more than half of the content and led the work. Describe what you actually did, rather than reading it off the byline.

Two consequences catch our students:

- **A genuinely equal paper cannot go in as a chapter.** If you and a coauthor each did half, neither of you contributed more than 50%. Write it up as your own chapter instead, acknowledging what each of you did.
- **Two PhD students cannot both use the same paper as a chapter.** Where two HDR candidates want to include the same coauthored work, both must present it as an ordinary chapter, with the contributions spelled out.

## Linking text

A paper dropped into a thesis does not explain itself, so linking text around it is required, not optional. The usual shape of a chapter built around a paper is:

```
Chapter 4   Paper 1
   4.1   How the paper fits the thesis
   4.2   The paper
   4.3   The results in the context of the thesis
```

Use those sections for what the paper had to leave out: methods a journal made you compress, results you cut for length, work published since, and a frank account of the limitations. If your examiners ask for changes to a chapter of this kind, you answer them in the linking text rather than by editing the paper.

The thesis as a whole still needs its own introduction, chapters in a logical sequence, a discussion that pulls the findings together, and a conclusion. UNSW allows a thesis **with** publications, not a thesis **by** publication: papers bound together are not a thesis.

## Acknowledging coauthored work

Whatever the category, name your coauthors and say what each of you contributed, at the start of the chapter or in a footnote, and get their permission in writing — including your supervisor's.

The Business School suggests wording along these lines for work that has not been published. Adapt it, fill in what each of you did, and say only what is true of your paper:

```
This chapter comprises a working paper presented at [conference] in [month,
year]. It is coauthored with [names]. I am the primary author and contributed
more than 50% of the work. [What you did; what each coauthor did.] I have
written permission from my coauthors to include this work in my thesis.
```

```
This chapter is based on unpublished work with [names]. I contributed more
than 50% of the work presented in this chapter. [What you did; what each
coauthor did.] I have written permission from my coauthors to include this
work in my thesis.
```

If a paper was presented at a conference that publishes no proceedings, give the conference and the date. If those sentences do not describe your paper, do not use them — ask the PGC what the chapter should say instead.

Changes a publisher's editors made to a chapter you have published count as editorial assistance, and must be disclosed in your acknowledgements along with any use of AI. See [thesis submission](/research-resources/thesis-submission/).

## What you declare at submission

Everyone completes the Inclusion of Publications Statement in GRIS, including candidates with no publications. For each paper you are including in place of a chapter, have ready:

- its title, authors, and the journal, book or series;
- volume and pages, and the date accepted or published, where they exist — a paper under review simply has none;
- its status: published, in press, or submitted;
- a description of your contribution, in your own words;
- where the work sits in the thesis, for example "this working paper is Chapter 2".

Separately, list every publication and presentation that came out of your research in the front matter of the thesis. That list and the GRIS statement are both required, and neither replaces the other.

At the oral examination the panel will ask you to account for your own contribution to any paper included in place of a chapter, and for what your coauthors contributed.
